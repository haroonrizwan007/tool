Large images are the most common reason pages feel slow. The good news is that most photos can lose more than half their weight before anyone notices a difference.

## 1. Resize before you compress

A photo straight from a phone is often 4000 pixels wide, while a blog column rarely shows more than 1200. Reducing the pixel dimensions removes data that was never visible. This single step usually saves more than any quality setting.

## 2. Pick the right format

Photographs belong in JPG or WebP. WebP typically produces files 25 to 35 percent smaller than JPG at similar quality. Keep PNG for screenshots, logos and anything with transparency.

## 3. Choose a sensible quality value

Quality around 70 to 80 is the sweet spot for photographs. Below 50, blocky artifacts start to appear around edges and gradients. Compare the result at full size before you commit.

## 4. Compress privately

Many online compressors upload your files to a server. The [Image Compressor](/image-compressor) on this site runs in your browser, so photos of documents, people or products never leave your device.

## A simple workflow

1. Resize to the largest width you will actually display.
2. Export as WebP or JPG at quality 75.
3. Check the file size and zoom in on detailed areas.
4. If it looks soft, raise quality by 5 and try again.
